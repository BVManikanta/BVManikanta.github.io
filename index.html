Fractional Optimal Control via the Reflection Operator

A direct, non-iterative way to solve a fractional-order two-point boundary value problem: reflect the right-sided derivative into a left-sided one, discretise with Grünwald–Letnikov, and solve one linear system for the optimal state and control.

1 solveOptimal state and control from a single linear system of 2N equations
6 ordersFractional orders α = 0.2 to 1.0 compared, up to the classical case
≈16% lessInitial control effort at α = 0.2 than at α = 1.0 (read from the simulation)
Hybrid dynamicsInteger- and fractional-order derivatives in the same constraint

The problem

Many real systems have memory, which fractional-order derivatives capture better than integer-order ones. Optimal control of such systems leads to necessary conditions with a left-sided derivative on the state and a right-sided derivative on the co-state: a two-point boundary value problem that is hard to solve directly.

min J = ½ ∫₀¹ ( q·x² + r·u² ) dt
s.t. ẋ + D^α x = a·x + b·u,  x(0) = 1,  x(1) free
D^α: left Riemann–Liouville derivative, order α ≤ 1

My approach

  1. Formulate. Build the Hamiltonian and augmented cost, take the first variation and use fractional integration by parts to get the state, co-state and control equations.
  2. Reflect. Apply the reflection operator, R f(t) = f(1 − t), so the right-sided derivative of the co-state becomes a left-sided one and both equations take the same form.
  3. Discretise. Split [0, 1] into N intervals and replace each Riemann–Liouville derivative with its Grünwald–Letnikov approximation, giving 2N algebraic equations.
  4. Solve. One linear solve returns the optimal state x(t) and control u(t). No iteration is needed.

What I found

  • Tested on a scalar example (a = 1, b = 2, q = 3, r = 1, N = 5) for α from 0.2 to 1.0.
  • Lower α needs less control effort. Control amplitude shrinks as the fractional order falls: the initial control is about −3.8 at α = 0.2 against about −4.5 at α = 1.
  • Boundary conditions hold as designed: x(0) = 1 is met and the control goes to zero at the free final time.
  • Next step: extend the scheme to time-varying problems.

Optimal Tuning of PSS and TCSC Stabilizer for Power System Stability

A TCSC damping controller on a single-machine infinite-bus system, tuned with Particle Swarm Optimization, that settles low-frequency oscillations in seconds instead of tens of seconds.

6–7× fasterSpeed oscillations settle in about 6 s with TCSC against 38–45 s without (exciter time-constant sweep)
≤ 7 sSettling time at all 45 operating points tested, against 17–76 s without TCSC
≈54% lowerPeak speed deviation with TCSC: 1.60×10⁻³ down to 0.73×10⁻³ pu
0Steady-state error with TCSC in every case

The problem

Rising demand and limited new transmission leave grids stressed. After a disturbance, low-frequency electromechanical oscillations can grow and threaten synchronism. A conventional power system stabilizer (PSS) damps well near its tuning point but can fall short across wide operating conditions. A TCSC, a series FACTS device, adds damping through the transmission line itself.

My approach

  1. Model. Linearised Phillips–Heffron model of a synchronous generator on an infinite bus (K1–K6) with exciter and AVR, extended with the TCSC damping constants Kp, Kq and Kv.
  2. Controller. A TCSC damping controller built from a gain block, a washout filter and a two-stage lead–lag compensator.
  3. Optimise. Particle Swarm Optimization (20 particles, 100 generations, c₁ = c₂ = 2, inertia 0.9 → 0.4) searches the controller parameters to minimise an integral-squared-error index, reaching about 3.2×10⁻⁴.
  4. Validate. MATLAB/Simulink runs of speed, power-angle and terminal-voltage deviation with and without TCSC, sweeping exciter time constant, exciter gain, damping and voltage disturbance: 45 operating points in all.

What I found

  • Oscillations settle in roughly 4–7 s with TCSC, against 17–76 s without it, for speed, power angle and terminal voltage alike.
  • Peak deviations fall too. In the exciter time-constant sweep, speed deviation is about 54% lower, power angle about 22% and terminal voltage about 23%.
  • The improvement holds across every exciter, damping and disturbance setting tested, and the steady-state error goes to zero.
  • Next steps: adaptive or AI-based stabilizers, other FACTS devices (STATCOM, UPFC) and larger networks.
